\section{Comparison to xv6 on primitive operations}
We use open-source system xv6 (\co{https://github.com/k-mrm/xv6-aarch64?tab=readme-ov-file}) as our baseline for performance. We feel this
is a good choice because they have very distinct design philosophies, yet are both teaching operating systems.
This section compares the latencies in cycles for three OS operations: vspace management,
process creation, and inter-process communication. \newline

\noindent For each subsystem we run the same benchmark on both systems and measure raw counter
ticks from the ARM generic timer (\co{cntvct\_el0}), reporting the mean and standard
deviation across iterations. The virtual memory benchmarks map regions of increasing
size, then measure first-touch page-fault latency and warm reaccess cost per page.
The process creation benchmark times a full spawn-and-wait cycle using a minimal
no-op child, isolating process lifecycle overhead from application work. The IPC
benchmarks measure round-trip latency by sending a single byte and waiting for a
reply, and measure bulk throughput by transferring a fixed number of bytes and
dividing by the elapsed cycle count.

\subsection{VSpace Management}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{fig/bench_vm.png}
    \caption{Results of virtual memory microbenchmarks.}
    \label{fig:vmbench}
\end{figure}

\noindent The most distinct result is the allocation cost. AOS remains
approximately constant costing $2{,}600$--$3{,}300$ cycles regardless of how many pages
are requested, while xv6 scales linearly from $1{,}700$ cycles for one page to
$271{,}775$ cycles for 256 pages. xv6 costs nearly 160x more at this large size.
Page-fault handling on AOS stabilises at roughly 19 cycles per page across all
region sizes. On xv6 the per-page fault cost collapses to near zero for regions
larger than four pages. We think this is due to xv6 eagerly mapping pages. \newline 

\noindent The constant allocation cost in AOS follows directly from the design of
\co{paging_map_frame}: it installs page-table entries covering the entire requested
virtual range in a single kernel operation, independent of the number of pages
involved. xv6 instead loops over every page calling \co{uvmalloc} which accounts for
its linear scaling. The page fault handling result is due to our system using demand
paging where xv6 use eager paging.


\subsection{Process Creation}
We test the latency of creating a process by 

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{fig/bench_proc.png}
    \caption{Results of process creation microbenchmark.}
    \label{fig:pcbench}
\end{figure}

\noindent The results show that process creation is 12x slower on barrelfish compared to xv6.
We suspect that this is due to RPC and capability overhead as well as ELF loading. xv6 \co{fork} simply
has to copy the kernel proc struct and shallow clone the page tables. The \co{exec} step adds ELF loading, which is the
only phase comparable to AOS's work, yet the full \co{fork}+\co{exec}+\co{wait} cycle
still finishes in $134{,}831$ cycles which is roughly the time AOS spends just on the RPC
protocol overhead.


\subsection{Inter-process Communication}

\begin{figure}[H]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{fig/bench_ipc.png}
    \caption{Results of process creation microbenchmark.}
    \label{fig:ipcbench}
\end{figure}

AOS LMP round-trip latency ($4{,}715$ cycles) is $3.6\times$ lower than xv6 pipe
round-trip ($16{,}987$ cycles). We see even better gains when processes are connected 
via UMP, aos sees $3.1$ cycles per byte communicated, versus $40.6$ cycles per byte for the xv6 bulk pipe transfer, a
$13\times$ advantage. \newline

\noindent We hypothesize these results stem from the number of kernel boundary crossings each
mechanism requires. The xv6 pipe round-trip involves two full context switches through
the kernel scheduler per message, whereas AOS LMP delivers synchronously to init on the
same core, bypassing the general scheduler entirely. UMP performs even better because it
requires zero boundary crossings and is simply memory fences and loads/stores.

\section{Implications for our design goals}
In the system overview section we state our main design goal as allowing as much concurrency
as possible. The third test as well as the microbenchmarks from the \hyperref[sec:performance-opt]{m4} and \hyperref[sec:ump-concurrent]{m6} sections,
show that our system is capable of supporting concurrent processes and threads performantly and
specifically that our RPC and IPC systems are robust to such environments. By allowing threads to have 
their own LMP channels and UMP servers to handle concurrent requests we extract as much
concurrency as feasible given the synchrony and lack of parallelism in these single core benchmarks. One especially disappointing 
result is the high cost to spawn a process, we think that this is inherent to the system, but wish to have
implemented spawning more performantly. Overall we feel as though the high pagefault and spawn latencies
are an acceptable tradeoff for increased concurrency and lazy commitment of resources.
